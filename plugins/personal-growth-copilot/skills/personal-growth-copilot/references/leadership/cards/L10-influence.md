# L10 — Influence through evidence and consultation

**Job:** seek a useful decision or commitment when you need another party's cooperation and cannot simply direct the work.

**Basis:** draft synthesis from Tradecraft-derived material authorized for private, noncommercial use, [Yukl and Tracey's influence-tactics research](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.77.4.525), and programme learning. The original field study used questionnaires from managers' colleagues; the associations support investigating persuasion and consultation, not a causal guarantee or this exact sequence. See the research update (source locator: research/leadership-toolbox/research-2026-10-03.md).

**Allow:** 15 minutes preparation and a 20-minute conversation, plus agreed follow-up; author estimates. Include the decision owner and people who bear the cost. Consultation must leave something meaningful open to change.

## Quick use

**When:** another party's cooperation is needed and a meaningful choice remains open.

**Opening:** “The outcome I hope to improve is ___. What matters from your side, and what would you change or reject?”

1. Identify the decision owner, affected interests and effort; ask the counterpart to correct your understanding.
2. Present evidence, uncertainty, costs and alternatives; invite changes that can alter the proposal.
3. Seek one proportionate commitment and return the agreed, declined or unresolved disposition for correction.

**Watch:** decision quality, actual changes from consultation and effort imposed on others. For a fixed decision, consult only on choices still open; repeated refusal is not permission to increase pressure.

## Reach for this when

## Switch or stop

If the decision is already fixed, explain it and consult only on what remains open. Do not call this co-creation. If capacity is absent, use [L09](L09-capacity.md); if objectives genuinely conflict, use [L04](L04-negotiation.md). Stop when participation exposes someone to retaliation or when a formal decision route is needed. Repeated refusal is not permission to increase pressure or recruit others to isolate the person.

## Run it

1. Write the decision sought, who owns it, who pays the effort/cost and what you can offer within your authority.
2. Ask the counterpart to explain their objectives and constraints. Check your summary with them before arguing for a solution.
3. Present the relevant evidence, uncertainty, effort and strongest objection. Explain the problem in their terms without concealing material consequences.
4. Invite a real change: “What would you change or reject in this proposal?” Include an alternative and the option to decline or defer.
5. Separate a shared interest from an unresolved tradeoff. If there is no workable overlap, record the disagreement and seek the appropriate decision rather than manufacturing consensus.
6. Ask for one proportionate commitment: reviewing an example, supplying missing evidence, identifying a sponsor or authorizing a bounded trial. Do not commit someone else's time.
7. Record what was agreed, declined or left open, the owner and the next check. Return the record for correction; silence does not mean acceptance.

**Output:** decision request, affected interests, evidence and costs, changes made through consultation, remaining disagreement and an explicit disposition.

## Fictional example

A practice lead wants a project manager to try an interface register. The manager fears another reporting task. The lead asks where coordination currently causes rework, shows one existing handover gap and proposes replacing one duplicated log for a single work package. The manager asks to retain the current approval route and limit preparation effort. Those become conditions of the proposal. A trial still needs the relevant owners' agreement. Refusal would remain a valid result.

## Learn from use

Look for a decision people understand, a material change made because of consultation, or a useful reason to stop. Track effort shifted to others and whether people felt able to disagree. Persuasion success is not proof of decision quality. Use the [practice record](../../behavior-change-experiments.md), including rejected proposals.

## Prepare a technical decision for leadership

For an architecture issue, prepare the problem and intended outcome, the plausible options, the technical and business consequences, the recommendation and the explicit decision sought. State assumptions and evidence limitations, and explain what further inquiry could reduce material uncertainty. Ask someone familiar with the audience to challenge the draft before the meeting.

Muller’s boardroom preparation supplies the problem/options/decision structure (2011 text, PDF pp. 200–202, E003 in the package (source locator: research/leadership-toolbox/books/systems-architecting-business-perspective/compilation/chapters/02-apply-with-boundaries.md)). Hale adds evidence and uncertainty communication; Larson adds a structured opening and early feedback in the NASA and Staff Engineer notes (source locator: research/leadership-toolbox/technical-leadership/README.md). This paragraph is a local synthesis. Preserve technical dissent and required escalation; adapting to an audience does not mean concealing a risk or assuming the recipient lacks expertise.
