# L04 — Interest-based negotiation

**Job:** find an acceptable agreement when parties have different demands and neither can simply direct the other.

**Basis:** the [Harvard Program on Negotiation explanation of principled negotiation](https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/negotiation-skills-daily/principled-negotiation-focus-interests-create-value/), drawing on *Getting to Yes*. It distinguishes people/problems, interests/positions, option generation and agreed criteria, and discusses alternatives to agreement. This is practitioner guidance, not proof that collaboration resolves every conflict. The worksheet and example are our adaptation.

**Allow:** 20–40 minutes preparation, a 30–60 minute discussion and authorization/follow-through as needed; author estimates.

## Quick use

**When:** parties need to resolve competing demands and have room to negotiate.

**Opening:** “What does each of us need to protect, and which parts can change?”

1. Clarify interests, authority, constraints and feasible alternatives if no agreement is reached.
2. Develop packages and compare them using agreed criteria, including effects on absent parties.
3. Record authorized terms, conditions, owners and review; retain no agreement when no acceptable option exists.

**Watch:** whether commitments happen and whose burden increases. Do not bargain away mandatory protections or another owner's resources; constrained participation may need a different forum.

## Reach for this when

Parties can discuss a real tradeoff, have some discretion and need an agreement. Influence comes from understanding the other party's work and making a credible proposal, rather than treating them as an obstacle to overcome.

## Switch or stop

Do not bargain away mandatory protections, promise another party's resources or misrepresent alternatives. A supposed shared interest may be your assumption. When participation is coerced or retaliation is plausible, an ordinary negotiation may be inappropriate. Use a protected or mediated route. If the problem is simply unclear ownership, start with [L01](L01-delegation.md).

## Run it

1. Define the decision, participants' authority and what happens if no agreement is reached. A fallback must be feasible, not a bluff.
2. List your requested position and the need behind it. Ask the other party to explain theirs; confirm your summary.
3. Distinguish shared interests, compatible differences and genuine conflicts. Ask what constraints cannot move and why.
4. Generate several packages before committing: scope, sequence, timing, resources or review arrangements can change together.
5. Agree relevant criteria and inspect the evidence. A benchmark selected only because it favours you is not neutral.
6. Compare the proposed package with feasible alternatives and its effects on absent parties. Pause for approval where required.
7. Record the decision, conditional terms, owners, review point and unresolved issues. An agreeable meeting is not authorization.

## Fictional example

An integration team wants an extra workshop; an operations team declines. Inquiry reveals a need for unresolved access decisions on one side and a staffing constraint on the other. They consider a full workshop, a short decision clinic and asynchronous review. A possible agreement uses pre-circulated questions and a 30-minute clinic with an authorized delegate. No live-work approval is inferred from that coordination agreement.

## Learn from use

Track whether commitments are authorized and carried out, whose burden increased and whether excluded interests later reopen the agreement. Preserve “no acceptable agreement” as a legitimate result. A common misuse is assuming a pleasant interaction means genuine consent. Use the [practice record](../../behavior-change-experiments.md).
