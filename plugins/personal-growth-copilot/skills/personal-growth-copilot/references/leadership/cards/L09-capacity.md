# L09 — Capacity tradeoff conversation

**Job:** turn competing commitments into an explicit priority or resource decision before the conflict is hidden in extra work.

**Basis:** local synthesis combining [interest-based negotiation](https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/negotiation-skills-daily/principled-negotiation-focus-interests-create-value/) with the resource/role clarification in [AHRQ's brief](https://www.ahrq.gov/teamstepps-program/curriculum/team/tools/briefs.html). The commitments table and process below are our design. No empirical evaluation of this combined protocol is claimed.

**Allow:** 10–20 minutes to assemble commitments and 15–30 minutes with the person who can prioritize or resource them; author estimates.

## Quick use

**When:** commitments exceed available capacity and a priority or resource decision is needed.

**Opening:** “Adding this requires changing the date, scope or support for something else. Which outcome should take priority?”

1. List commitments, due windows, minimum outcomes and realistic delivery, review and support effort.
2. Offer feasible choices to the authority holder: keep, change, defer, transfer with resources or decline.
3. Record the authorized tradeoff, notify affected parties and check actual workload and quality.

**Watch:** whether work is removed or resourced, including hidden hours and the recipient's load. Keep an unresolved conflict visible; coaching cannot supply missing capacity.

## Reach for this when

A new request conflicts with existing commitments, or repeated out-of-hours work masks an unresolved demand/capacity gap. Treat it as a work-design question before assuming weak resilience, poor motivation or a need for another productivity technique.

## Switch or stop

Do not unilaterally cancel another party's commitment or delay an urgent protective action. Clarify the immediate response first, then resolve the continuing load. If estimates are uncertain, show ranges and assumptions. If the apparent gap is missing skill or tools, address that cause explicitly.

## Run it

1. List existing commitments, due windows, minimum acceptable outcomes and resource assumptions. Include review and coordination effort.
2. Describe the new request and the consequence of accepting it unchanged. Use actual evidence; avoid claiming a precise utilization percentage from guesses.
3. Prepare feasible alternatives: defer a deliverable, narrow scope with approval, transfer work with resources, obtain support, or decline the new request.
4. Ask the authority holder to compare consequences. Suggested language: “I can deliver A and B within the current capacity. Adding C requires changing the date, scope or support for one of them. Which outcome should take priority?”
5. Record the agreed tradeoff, owner and affected-party notifications. If no decision is made, preserve the unresolved conflict rather than silently promising everything.
6. Check actual workload and quality after the change. Confirm the burden was removed or resourced rather than displaced to someone less able to refuse.

**Output:** a commitment disposition: keep/change/defer/transfer/decline, with who authorized it and who needs to know.

## Fictional example

A team lead receives a request for a new client briefing while two reviews are due. They identify an existing briefing that can be adapted, a lower-priority internal report that could move, and the reviewer capacity needed. The manager selects the adapted briefing and approves moving the internal report. The lead confirms that the report recipient accepts the revised date. The outcome is a transparent tradeoff; it is not a claim that the workload problem has permanently disappeared.

## Learn from use

Observe whether priorities actually changed, whether hidden hours fell and whether quality or another person's load deteriorated. A common failure is a nominal delegation that leaves all checking, rescue and deadline pressure with the original owner. Use the [practice record](../../behavior-change-experiments.md).
