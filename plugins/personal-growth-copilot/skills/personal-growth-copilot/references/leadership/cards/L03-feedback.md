# L03 — SBI feedback with inquiry about intent

**Job:** discuss a particular behaviour and its effect without pretending to know the other person's motives.

**Basis:** Center for Creative Leadership's [Situation–Behavior–Impact and intent inquiry (SBII)](https://www.ccl.org/articles/leading-effectively-articles/closing-the-gap-between-intent-vs-impact-sbii/), published August 19, 2025. CCL describes benefits and research support; this pass verified the public method description, not an independent causal evaluation of the branded script. The agreement and follow-up steps below are local extensions.

**Allow:** 5–10 minutes to separate observation from interpretation, then 10–20 minutes privately; author estimates. Use for reinforcing useful behaviour as well as correction.

## Quick use

**When:** a specific observed behaviour needs discussion and the person can respond freely.

**Opening:** “At ___, I observed ___. The effect I noticed was ___. What was happening from your side?”

1. Describe the situation, behaviour and observed impact privately, separating interpretation.
2. Hear intent and context; correct your account or pause to check disputed facts.
3. Agree a next behaviour and support when possible; record disagreement or referral accurately and revisit a later example.

**Watch:** whether the account was corrected, behaviour changed and speaking up remained possible. Use a protected process for sensitive allegations; silence is not agreement.

Read the stop conditions below before acting. ## Reach for this when

You can identify a specific event and observed behaviour, and the person has a fair opportunity to respond. The mechanism is specificity plus inquiry: both people can inspect the account instead of debating a personality label.

## Switch or stop

If the event is disputed, gather facts and hear the account before drawing a conclusion. For harassment, retaliation or a formal investigation, use the appropriate protected process. Publicly confronting someone who cannot safely respond may amplify harm. For a resource disagreement, [L04](L04-negotiation.md) may fit better.

## Run it

1. Choose a private, appropriate moment and explain the topic.
2. State the **situation** precisely enough that the person can identify it.
3. Describe observable **behaviour**. Replace “you were dismissive” with what was said or done; do not invent exact quotations.
4. Explain the **impact** you observed. Separate your experience from inferred effects on others: “I lost track of the unanswered issue” is different from “everyone lost confidence.”
5. Ask about **intent** and context. Listen, then revise your interpretation if the evidence warrants it. Good intent need not erase a harmful effect.
6. Agree one next behaviour and any support or environmental change. Ask how both of you will recognize it.
7. Revisit a later example, including when the behaviour helped.

## Fictional example

“At Tuesday's review, you moved to the next topic while the maintainer was still describing the access constraint. We closed the item without that constraint being recorded. What were you trying to achieve at that point?” The chair explains the time pressure. They agree that the chair will either capture the unfinished point or assign a follow-up before closing. The output is a checked account and a specific change, not a label such as “poor listener.”

## Learn from use

Check whether the account was corrected, the next behaviour was understood and a later instance changed. Watch for reduced speaking-up or compliance without understanding. The failure mode is a polished accusation delivered through an acronym. Use the [practice record](../../behavior-change-experiments.md).

## Check the leader's account too

Ask “What have I missed or misunderstood?” before seeking a behaviour agreement. If the material facts remain contested, record the accounts and the next evidence to check; do not write agreement merely because the more junior person goes quiet. End or defer the conversation when it becomes coercive or needs a protected route. A manager's legitimate instruction can be stated as an instruction, with its basis; it should not be disguised as voluntary agreement.

**Closure:** record heard, disputed, action decided or referred elsewhere, without equating these with agreement. Respect a request to pause the discussion; any necessary operational decision remains with its actual owner and is recorded separately.

## Prepare a difficult conversation

When the disagreement reaches beyond one observable behaviour, prepare three separate notes: what happened and what is disputed; your feelings; and what the situation seems to threaten about your identity. Treat the other person's experience as something to ask about. Start with a difference both parties can recognize: “We seem to have different understandings of what was agreed. Can we compare them and work out the next step?” The wording is our adaptation.

Separate intent from impact. Explore how the situation arose and what can change, without assuming equal responsibility or abandoning a necessary investigation. **Source:** Stone, Patton and Heen, *Difficult Conversations*, third edition (2023), chapters 1, 3, 4 and 8; source notes (source locator: research/leadership-toolbox/books/difficult-conversations-2023/compilation/BOOK_OVERVIEW.md), E001–E004.

## Receive feedback as well as give it

1. Clarify the purpose: appreciation, coaching or evaluation. Ask for a concrete example and the expectation it relates to.
2. Notice whether your reaction concerns the content, the giver/relationship, or what the feedback means about you. These are prompts for your reflection, not labels for the other person.
3. If the delivery is also a problem, name both topics and agree how to address each. Do not let the delivery concern silently replace the content, or let the content excuse disrespect.
4. Decide what to test, query, decline or defer. State a needed pause or boundary while keeping legitimate work duties and formal processes explicit.

**Opening:** “I want to understand this. Is this advice on improving, or an evaluation against an expectation? What example would help me see the gap?”

**Source:** Stone and Heen, *Thanks for the Feedback* (2014), chapters 1, 2, 5 and 10; patterns and limits (source locator: research/leadership-toolbox/books/thanks-for-the-feedback/compilation/patterns.md), E001–E004.
