# L11 — Check perspectives and missing evidence

**Job:** discover whether different information, assumptions or objectives explain a disagreement before choosing an intervention.

**Allow:** 5 minutes individual preparation and 15–25 minutes together; author estimates. Use private or written input where appropriate. Do not promise anonymity in a small team when identities may be apparent.

## Quick use

**When:** different information or assumptions may explain a problem or disagreement.

**Opening:** “What did you observe, what do you infer, and what might I be missing?”

1. State the decision and what is open; obtain accounts independently where useful and allow separate input.
2. Separate observations, interpretations and unknowns; examine evidence against competing explanations, including your own.
3. Decide the next inquiry or action, retain dissent and name the evidence that would change the framing.

**Watch:** whether relevant information changes the decision without turning inquiry into interrogation. Retrieve a single missing record directly when enough; use protected routes for sensitive allegations. Imagined stakeholder views are not evidence.

## Reach for this when

People describe the same event differently, the leader is confident without hearing the work-level account, or a decision repeatedly stalls despite apparent agreement. The aim is to expose decision-relevant differences; it is not to make every perspective equally well supported.

## Switch or stop

An active incident may need immediate authorized action. Sensitive allegations need a protected process. Do not make people defend personal experiences in a public fact-checking exercise. If facts are already shared and the real issue is competing interests, move to [L04](L04-negotiation.md). If the only missing item is an accessible record, retrieve it instead of convening a workshop.

## Run it

1. State the decision and deadline, the person who decides, and what remains genuinely open.
2. Before sharing a preferred answer, ask each participant independently: “What did you directly observe? What do you infer? What information might the others not have? What matters to you here?” Offer a way to pass or contribute separately.
3. Record each consequential claim with its source/date. Separate observation, interpretation, preference and unknown. Count evidence sources rather than repeated endorsements of the same source.
4. Ask whose perspective is absent and whether that omission could change the decision. Do not invent that person's motives or consent.
5. Compare at least two plausible explanations. Ask what observation would count against each, including the leader's preferred explanation. Apply the same scrutiny to all accounts.
6. Choose the next job: retrieve a fact, clarify an objective, resolve an authority/capacity conflict, test an uncertain effect, or decide using the available evidence. Set an inquiry deadline proportionate to the decision; do not defer indefinitely.
7. Record which input changed the framing, remaining dissent and the evidence that would reopen the decision. Return summaries of people's views for correction.

**Output:** a short table of claim, observation/source, interpretation, missing evidence and consequence for the choice. Finish with one decision or named next inquiry.

## Fictional example

A sponsor believes a team is resisting a new process; a team member says two reporting deadlines conflict. Separate accounts reveal that the sponsor saw the new priority but not the unchanged old commitment. The next action is a capacity/priority decision using L09, followed by L06 to reconnect the team. If both deadlines were already reconciled, that explanation would weaken and another inquiry would be needed. No improvement is claimed from the illustration.

## Learn from use

Observe whether new relevant information changed the framing or decision, and what remained unresolved. Check whether the exercise became an interrogation, privileged the loudest voice or created excessive delay. Agreement and speaking time are insufficient outcome measures. Use the [practice record](../../behavior-change-experiments.md).

An AI helper may propose alternative explanations and questions. Its imagined stakeholder views are hypotheses to check, never participant testimony or additional independent evidence. Do not start a separate agent or monitoring process merely to use this card.

## Ask from genuine curiosity

Before asking, identify what you genuinely do not know and what remains open to change. State fixed constraints plainly. Ask, listen and play back the account for correction before proposing your explanation. Question wording cannot compensate for a decision already made and disguised as inquiry.

Use Schein's ORJI distinction on your own reasoning: **observation → reaction → judgment → intervention**. Separate what you noticed from what you felt and inferred; consider what would change the judgment. This is reflection, not a test for diagnosing somebody else.

If the interaction stops helping, make a process inquiry: “Is this conversation helping us understand the issue? What would make it easier to continue?” A pause or different setting may be useful. In remote conversations, clarify context and respect privacy rather than reading engagement from a camera or background.
