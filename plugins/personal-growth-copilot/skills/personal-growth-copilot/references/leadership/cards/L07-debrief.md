# L07 — Structured debrief

**Job:** turn a completed event into a specific change in the next attempt.

**Basis:** [AHRQ TeamSTEPPS debrief guidance](https://www.ahrq.gov/teamstepps-program/curriculum/team/tools/debrief.html), reviewed December 2023, with empirical context from [Tannenbaum and Cerasoli, 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23516804/). Their meta-analysis abstract reports 46 samples and 2,136 participants, supporting an average benefit for debriefing across included studies. Full study-level methods were not reviewed in this pass; the abstract does not validate this exact adaptation or predict a benefit for a particular team.

**Allow:** 5 minutes preparation, 15–25 minutes for a bounded event and a check on the next occurrence; author estimates. AHRQ also describes very short debriefs. Participants need relevant first-hand accounts, not just seniority.

## Quick use

**When:** a completed episode offers evidence for improving the next occurrence.

**Opening:** “What did we expect, what happened, and what should we keep or change next time?”

1. Bound the event and explain how the discussion will be used; hear records and first-hand accounts.
2. Compare expectations with observations and examine more than one plausible cause.
3. Agree one or two changes, owners and a next observation while retaining effective practice and unresolved differences.

**Watch:** whether changes are used and what happens on recurrence. Stabilize ongoing incidents first; keep formal investigations and protected disclosures in their appropriate process.

## Reach for this when

The team has an identifiable event, some evidence of what happened and a chance to change future work. The mechanism connects intended performance, observed differences and a testable adjustment. A debrief can examine success as well as failure.

## Switch or stop

Stabilize any ongoing incident first. Preserve formal reporting and investigation obligations. Do not promise confidentiality you cannot provide, pressure people to reveal protected information, or turn a learning discussion into an undeclared disciplinary hearing. Where status differences inhibit candour, use an appropriate facilitator or another evidence-gathering route.

## Run it

1. Bound the event and name the intended result. Explain how the discussion will be used.
2. Reconstruct what happened using records and participants' accounts. Keep unresolved differences visible.
3. Compare intention and observation: what helped, what hindered and which expectations were wrong?
4. Explore reasons without making the first explanation the verdict. Consider roles, resources, information, tools and decisions as well as individual actions.
5. Select one or two changes that address a supported cause. Give each an owner and a next occasion for checking it.
6. Preserve effective practice too. Summarize what is known, what remains uncertain and what needs another process.
7. At the next occurrence, check whether the change was used and what happened. A lesson entered into a log has not yet changed practice.

## Fictional example

A review pack was issued late. The team initially attributes it to slow checking. The timeline shows that the input revision changed after checking began. They agree to label the input baseline at kickoff and flag subsequent changes explicitly. On the next package they will check rework time and missed changes. The proposed action addresses an observed coordination problem; it does not establish that the checker caused the delay.

## Learn from use

Track enacted changes, recurrence and unintended effects. “More lessons captured” is not the success measure. A common failure is a discussion that ends with “communicate better” and no owner or next observation. Use the [practice record](../../behavior-change-experiments.md).
