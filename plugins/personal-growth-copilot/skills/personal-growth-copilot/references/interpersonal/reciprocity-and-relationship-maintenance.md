# Relationship Norms, Reciprocity, and Maintenance

Use this reference when the user asks whether a friendship, family, romantic,
professional, household, or other ongoing relationship is one-sided; how much
to invest; how to maintain connection; whether to keep helping; or how to
respond to repeated unmet commitments. Add the romance and intimacy reference
when attraction, exclusivity, sexual boundaries, breakup, or reconciliation is
material.

## Classify the legitimate relationship norm first

Do not apply one reciprocity model to every relationship. Identify the best
supported norm:

- **communal care or need response:** help may follow need rather than immediate
  equivalent return;
- **explicit shared arrangement:** duties concern money, space, schedules,
  caregiving, or recurring work;
- **professional exchange:** expectations follow role, deliverable, access,
  credit, or development;
- **voluntary social or romantic connection:** obligation follows the actual
  agreement; contribution forms may differ;
- **mixed or disputed norm:** participants appear to expect different types of
  relationship.

Ask what obligation is explicit, reasonably shared, voluntary where choice
exists, sustainable, and revisable. Do not turn friendship, romance, or temporary care
into a ledger. Do not use communal language to hide chronic unagreed burden.

## Review events, not personal worth

Across a meaningful window examine:

- reliability and early warning;
- responsiveness to material needs or commitments;
- initiative appropriate to the relationship norm;
- burden in time, money, emotional labor, access, and risk;
- repair after misses;
- respect for limits and refusal;
- legitimate value to each person, even when contributions differ;
- renegotiability as capacity changes.

Temporary asymmetry may be legitimate during illness, crisis, caregiving,
onboarding, sponsorship, or unequal formal roles. The issue is whether the
arrangement is understood, voluntary where possible, sustainable, and open to
revision.

## Relationship-Norm-Event-Pattern-Agreement

1. **Norm:** what type of obligation is legitimate here?
2. **Event:** what happened, when, and with what practical effect?
3. **Pattern:** isolated, situational, or repeated across comparable events?
4. **Expectation:** what was explicit or reasonably shared under this norm?
5. **Known context:** what constraint is known rather than inferred?
6. **Revision:** what future arrangement would be sustainable?
7. **Observation:** what behavior during what window would show the revision
   works?
8. **Decision:** maintain, narrow, pause, formalize, reduce dependency, or exit.

Output pattern:

> The current evidence supports ___. The relationship appears to operate more
> like ___ than ___. A sustainable revision is ___. Reassess after ___; if ___
> continues, reduce or change ___.

Do not ask whether the other person “really cares” when the practical question
is whether the observable arrangement works.

## Distinguish generosity from a hidden contract

Before helping, ask:

- Am I willing to do this if no equivalent favor returns?
- Have I stated any real limit or condition?
- Can the recipient decline without penalty?
- Does this displace another commitment or create resentment?
- Am I hoping the favor purchases access, affection, loyalty, silence,
  promotion, or pursuit?

If an exchange condition matters, state it truthfully and ethically. Do not give
freely in words and invoice the person emotionally later.

## Maintain professional and social connections

Use light, specific, non-extractive contact:

1. remember work, interests, or stated priorities in a user-controlled profile
   when useful, without hidden surveillance or manipulation fields;
2. share information or recognition when genuinely relevant;
3. make bounded requests with easy decline;
4. close the loop after advice or an introduction;
5. credit help accurately;
6. reconnect for a real reason rather than fake urgency.

Example:

> Your advice on ___ helped me change ___. The result was ___. Thank you. I also
> saw ___ and thought it might be relevant to your work; no response needed.

Healthy professional relationships may be low-frequency and context-specific.
Do not manufacture constant contact to simulate closeness.

## Handle repeated helping and rescue work

Identify:

- the immediate problem the rescue solves;
- the system, authority gap, or behavior it allows to continue;
- displaced work and exposure;
- whether help is emergency support, teaching, shared work, or ownership
  transfer;
- the named owner and end condition before helping again.

> I can help recover this handoff once today. I cannot remain the fallback
> owner. Before the next deadline, we need the named owner, warning point, and
> escalation route.

Helping becomes enabling when it repeatedly removes the operational feedback or
consequence needed for the real owner or system to change. This is a process
assessment, not a diagnosis of exploitation.

## Renegotiate uneven investment

Use:

1. a neutral description of the pattern;
2. the practical or relational impact;
3. curiosity about a known or discoverable constraint;
4. a narrower agreement suited to the relationship norm;
5. an observation window;
6. willingness to reduce dependency if the arrangement remains unsustainable.

Different contribution forms may be legitimate. A friend may initiate less but
provide meaningful support in other ways; a coworker's gratitude does not
replace ownership of recurring work.

## Manage triangulation

Before involving a third party, state the legitimate function:

- decision authority;
- missing information;
- mediation;
- representation or protection;
- verification;
- formal review.

Do not use allies for gossip, reputation warfare, secret pursuit tests, or
manufactured consensus. Indirect supported communication may be appropriate in
unsafe or high-power cases when its purpose and factual boundary are explicit.

## Reduce or exit investment

Consider narrowing or ending dependency when:

- important commitments repeatedly fail without warning or repair;
- boundaries trigger pressure, retaliation, or ridicule;
- the arrangement requires concealment or unethical conduct;
- chronic cost has no viable renegotiation;
- the relationship blocks safer or more sustainable alternatives;
- the agreed observation window ends without change.

Use proportionate, ownership-specific language:

> This arrangement is no longer workable for me. I will complete ___ by ___,
> then I will no longer own ___. Future requests need to go through ___.

Do not disappear as a secret test designed to provoke pursuit. Do not remove
material shared ownership without notice when safe notice is possible.

## Failure modes

- immediate tit-for-tat accounting;
- unsupported loyalty or influence scores, or relationship scores that hide
  evidence, unknowns, coverage, confidence, or user-chosen weights;
- interpreting different contribution forms as no contribution;
- treating caregiving or crisis asymmetry as exploitation;
- hiding a contract inside generosity;
- endless patience without a review window;
- using empathy to erase opportunity cost;
- manufacturing contact to seem close;
- recruiting allies to punish or isolate someone;
- covert withdrawal or delayed-response tests.

## Evidence boundary

Social-exchange and communal-versus-exchange research supports distinguishing
relationship norms and contingent obligations at a general level. It does not
classify a relationship, predict loyalty, or validate the product's Decision
Fit Score. Event-based observation, explicit renegotiation, user-controlled
scoring, and stop rules are product safeguards. See `EXCHANGE-01`,
`RELATION-01`, and `MEASURE-01` in the evidence ledger.
